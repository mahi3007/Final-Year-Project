2. The estimand is now mathematically clearer, with one important distinction

The report defines each speaker's WER as:

$$ \operatorname{WER}_{s,m}^{(a)} = \frac{S_{s,m}^{(a)}+D_{s,m}^{(a)}+I_{s,m}^{(a)}} {N_{s,\mathrm{ref}}} $$

where edit counts are accumulated over the speaker's eligible utterances.

It then averages the speaker-level contrasts equally across participants.

That is a defensible approach for a fairness-focused study because it prevents speakers with substantially more reference words from dominating the primary result.

However, it is important to describe the resulting quantity accurately.

Your primary estimand is an equal-speaker macro-average of per-speaker WER differences—not the difference in pooled corpus WER.

The distinction matters because conventional WER divides total word errors by the total number of reference words. Speaker-macro averaging instead gives every speaker equal weight after computing that speaker's WER. These quantities can differ when speakers contribute different amounts of speech.

I recommend retaining the proposed equal-speaker estimand if it reflects your research objective, while making the following definitions explicit:

WER is aggregated over each speaker's eligible utterances before the arm contrast is calculated.
Each speaker receives equal weight.
Each model receives equal weight only if the four-model amendment is approved.
Group contributions are determined by the prespecified sampling and weighting scheme.
The transcript normalization and reference-word rules remain fixed.
Stream-order effects are handled explicitly rather than omitted from the estimand.

The pooled-word contrast should remain a secondary descriptive result unless the supervisor approves a different primary target.

3. Critical blocker: The safety confidence bound is not yet fully specified

Your proposed safety statistic is:

$$ D_m^{(a)} = \max_{g\in\mathcal G}\operatorname{WER}_{g,m}^{(a)} - \min_{g\in\mathcal G}\operatorname{WER}_{g,m}^{(a)} $$

and:

$$ \Delta_D = D^{(3)}-D^{(2)} $$

where the model-level disparities are averaged across the proposed four models.

This is a plausible way to measure the change in the range of group-level WERs.

But three gaps remain.

A. Group-level WER needs a definition

The report does not fully specify whether \(\operatorname{WER}_{g,m}^{(a)}\) means:

The equal-speaker average of individual speaker WERs within group \(g\); or
Pooled-word WER across all speakers in group \(g\).

Those are different estimands. Given your primary equal-speaker objective, I would recommend evaluating whether the first is the appropriate choice for consistency, but that choice must be explicit and approved.

B. Averaging disparity across models can hide model-specific safety failures

Your proposed statistic is:

$$ D^{(a)} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}D_m^{(a)}. $$

This evaluates the average disparity across the selected models. A large disparity increase for one model could be offset by smaller decreases for other models.

If your claim is that the gate controls disparity for every model, this average does not establish that stronger claim.

The supervisor needs to choose between an average-model safety estimand and a safety requirement that also constrains adverse model-specific outcomes. The multiplicity and confidence-bound procedure must then match that decision.

C. Calling the bound “adjusted” requires further specification

The report proposes taking the 97.5th empirical percentile of \(B=1{,}000\) speaker-stratified bootstrap replicates.

That defines a percentile-bootstrap upper bound, but the report does not specify what statistical adjustment makes it an adjusted upper confidence bound.

With 1,000 bootstrap replicates, approximately 25 lie beyond the 97.5th percentile. This gives limited resolution for estimating such an upper-tail quantile.

Bootstrap inference also depends on resampling at a unit that respects the dependence structure. Standard cluster bootstrap methods resample clusters rather than treating every observation as independent; the appropriate choice depends on the actual design.

Required action: Specify the exact disparity statistic, the multiplicity family, the confidence-bound construction, and the resampling scheme. Do not use the label Adjusted UCB until the adjustment is defined.

The \(+1.40\)-percentage-point safety threshold must remain:

PROPOSED — NOT APPROVED AS A CONFIRMATORY SAFETY BOUNDARY

It is not an approved ADR-005 threshold merely because it provides a margin below the operational \(+2.00\)-point ceiling.

4. Critical blocker: The proposed bootstrap does not yet account for continual adaptation

Your report correctly recognizes that the model's state carries forward through the stream:

$$ \theta_{t+1} = \operatorname{Adapt}(\theta_t,B_t). $$

Consequently, a speaker's outcome may depend on the preceding sequence of speakers and model updates.

However, the report's proposed bootstrap resamples speakers and computes disparities on the resulting samples. That procedure does not automatically reproduce the trajectory dependence induced by continual adaptation.

There are two different operations:

Post-hoc resampling: Resample already computed speaker outcomes and recalculate the statistic.

Trajectory-aware resampling: Construct an appropriate resampled stream and replay the adaptation procedure so that each evaluation reflects the state resulting from its preceding sequence.

These procedures estimate uncertainty under different assumptions.

The current specification must not claim that resampling speaker-level outcomes automatically preserves the adaptation trajectory.

There is also a limitation with using only three prescribed orderings. Treating those three orderings as independent clusters would provide a very small number of clusters for conventional cluster-level inference. A bootstrap or mixed-effects model needs a justified dependence structure, not just a different statistical label.

My recommendation

Keep all three proposed inference approaches—stream-aware bootstrap, mixed-effects modelling and paired permutation—as candidates for methodological assessment.

Before selecting one, determine:

What population the inference is intended to generalize to.
Whether the inference is conditional on the three prescribed stream orderings or generalizes beyond them.
Whether the full adaptation procedure must be rerun in each resample.
Whether the proposed model adequately represents the sequential state dependence.
Whether the method satisfies the approved ADR-005 inferential requirements.

Do not declare the paired Student-t test definitively invalid solely because state carry-forward permits dependence. Instead, require evidence that its assumptions are appropriate—or select a defensible alternative before confirmatory execution.

This is internally coherent as a proposed operational status system.

where \(M\) is the number of models, 2 represents Arms 2 and 3, and \(\mathcal O\) denotes stream orderings.

The specification must define \(T\) precisely: it needs to be the total number of required evaluation windows per complete stream, not an ambiguously defined count per speaker or per ordering.

unless the integrity condition is modelled explicitly or the power quantity is clearly defined as conditional on complete evaluation.

The supervisor must decide whether integrity is:

An operational validity requirement separate from statistical power; or
A component of the simulated end-to-end joint success probability.

Do not mix those interpretations in the next power analysis.

6. Rule B: The proposed Monte Carlo precision plan is reasonable, but not a guarantee

Your current findings for the existing simulation remain:

\(N\)	Estimated joint power	Lower 95% Monte Carlo bound	Rule A	Rule B
450	84.50%	83.38%	Fail	Fail
468	85.825%	84.74%	Pass	Fail
540	90.40%	89.49%	Pass	Pass under baseline assumptions

Power estimated by simulation is the proportion of simulated datasets in which the specified analysis rejects its null hypothesis or satisfies its predefined success criterion. Its precision depends on the number of simulation replications and the variance of that estimated proportion.

Your proposed \(S=20{,}000\) setting is a plausible planning option. At a success probability near 85.8%, it should give a materially more precise estimate than \(S=4{,}000\).

But there are two important qualifications.

First, the claim that the margin of error will be at most \(0.50\) percentage points is conditional on the underlying simulated success probability being near the expected value. It is not a universal guarantee for every possible probability.

Second, a larger simulation may produce a lower 95% bound above 85%, but it cannot guarantee that result in advance. It will estimate the operating power more precisely; it does not increase the power of the physical experiment.

Most importantly, the simulation must be aligned with the final estimand and success event before the new value of \(S\) is chosen.

The current simulation uses a scalar Gaussian setup, whereas the proposed analysis introduces four-model aggregation, a different primary contrast, sequential dependence, and a new integrity condition.

The old power values are therefore useful for understanding their original setup, but they cannot be treated as power estimates for the new confirmatory design.

Rule B remains mandatory. The specification must also use the same strict inequality everywhere:

$$ \boxed{L_{95\%,\mathrm{MC}}>85\%} $$

not sometimes \(L_{95\%,\mathrm{MC}}\geq85\%\).

The proposed primary comparison is Arm 3 versus Arm 2.

Your report includes Arm 1 as a secondary reference, which is useful. However, if the thesis claims both drift mitigation and a net adaptation benefit, the professor must decide whether Arm 3 versus Arm 1 is a secondary analysis or part of the confirmatory success criteria.
